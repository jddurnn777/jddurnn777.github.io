Closed-Loop Leg Controls for a NASA Lunabotics Quadruped

Learning PID, quasi-direct drive, and whole-body control from a rope-powered robot dog on YouTube.
A note before this one: I'm on the Controls Engineering team for UCF's NASA Lunabotics excavation quadruped, working on the electrical schematic and controls for one leg. Lunabotics is a NASA-sponsored competition, and our team treats the specific design numbers — current limits, wire gauges, calibration values, that kind of thing — as internal to the team. So the schematic below has those blacked out, and I've kept the specific figures out of the writing too. Everything else here is exactly how I'd explain it to a friend.

What got me into this

Two videos got me oriented before I touched anything. The first was Brian Douglas' PID Control — A brief introduction, which is just a genuinely clear explanation of the three terms and why you'd want each one. The second was I Built a Robot Dog Using... Rope?, a build log for a robot called CARA. CARA ended up being the design philosophy I kept coming back to for our own leg.

PID control, the way I'd actually explain it

PID is just three ways of reacting to "how wrong am I right now, and how has that wrongness been behaving." Say a joint is supposed to hold a position and something nudges it off:

  • Proportional (P): the bigger the current error, the harder you push back toward the target. Simple, but push too hard and you overshoot. Push too soft and the joint just sags under load and never quite gets there.
  • Integral (I): keeps a running tally of error over time. This is what kills that lingering sag. If the joint's been a little off for a while, the integral term keeps nudging harder until it's actually zeroed out, not just close.
  • Derivative (D): looks at how fast the error is changing and pushes back against that. It's the term that keeps P from overshooting and oscillating. It sees the joint approaching the target fast and eases off before it flies past.

Tuning is just balancing those three against each other: enough P to respond quickly, enough I to kill steady-state error without getting sluggish, enough D to damp out oscillation without making the joint feel numb.

Whole-body control and the CARA design philosophy

CARA's build log is where the "quasi-direct drive" idea comes from, and it's the concept I think about most for our leg. A quasi-direct drive pairs a wide, high-torque "pancake" motor with a low gear ratio, low enough that the connection between motor and leg is almost direct. The point isn't speed, it's compliance. If the leg steps on something unexpected, it can flex and give way like a muscle absorbing an impact, instead of transmitting the full shock straight into a gearbox and snapping something. You still keep almost all of your control authority. You just also get some natural forgiveness built into the mechanics instead of relying entirely on software to react fast enough.

From there, CARA's control stack builds up in stages, and it's a clean mental model for whole-body control in general:

  1. Tune the motors so the joint has a natural, damped response to outside forces. That's the PID tuning above, applied per joint.
  2. Home each joint using a rise in motor current as the signal. Drive the joint until it hits a hard stop and current spikes, and now you know exactly where zero is without a separate position sensor.
  3. Derive the inverse kinematics for the leg geometry, so you can command the foot to a specific point in x, y, and z instead of commanding joint angles directly. This is the layer that lets you think "put the foot here" instead of "spin this motor this much."
  4. Plan a stepping trajectory. CARA uses a triangular trajectory for the foot path, constant acceleration up, constant deceleration down, rather than constant velocity, and it's noticeably less jerky. Same triangular profile gets applied independently on each axis.

Our leg uses a five-bar linkage for the mechanical geometry, which is a big part of why the inverse kinematics derivation isn't trivial. The foot's position is a function of two motor angles through a closed linkage, not a simple open chain.

The leg's electrical architecture

Each leg has three joints, each driven by an ODrive S1 controller running one of three control modes depending on what that joint needs: position, velocity, or torque control. A Teensy 4.1 is the leg's local microcontroller, talking to all three ODrives over CAN.

Why we ended up designing a PCB

The honest answer is signal integrity on the CAN bus. Once you're running several ODrives off one bus, you start worrying about how to catch corrupted data, and that pushed us into comparing error-detection methods: CRC, checksum, LRC (longitudinal redundancy check), and VRC (vertical redundancy check). We landed on CRC, the same kind of cyclic redundancy check cars use on their own CAN buses. It's a computed code appended to each message that lets the receiver detect whether the data changed in transit, without needing to resend anything to confirm it.

That decision fed into a bigger topology question — daisy chain vs. star:

  • Daisy chain: a break anywhere leaves both sides improperly terminated, and one failed connector can take down every node downstream of it. Intermittent wiring faults are genuinely tedious to isolate. In exchange, it supports much higher achievable speeds, roughly 1–5 Mbps.
  • Star: a fault on one spoke only takes out that one device, and the wiring itself is more intuitive. A central junction block with individual runs out to each node, so troubleshooting is modular. The tradeoff is a much lower achievable speed, typically under 125 kbps.

Since we're running the bus through a Jetson, we get to use CRC-based error detection, the same category of technique automotive CAN buses lean on at much higher data rates, which is part of why the topology tradeoff isn't as painful as it would be on a simpler embedded setup.

Sensing: why an IMU

Beyond joint-level control, the quadruped needs to know its own orientation, which means an IMU (inertial measurement unit): an accelerometer, gyroscope, and often a magnetometer bundled together. The accelerometer measures linear acceleration (how velocity is changing), and the gyroscope measures angular rate around each axis (how orientation is changing). Together those give the whole-body controller what it needs to know, not just where each foot is, but which way the whole body is tipping or turning.

A current challenge worth mentioning

One thing we're actively working through: the geometry of a curved foot is genuinely hard to control for, because the contact point and effective foot geometry keep changing as the foot rotates through a step. Our current plan is to use snowshoes and treat the foot as effectively flat, since a flat contact surface is dramatically easier to model and control than a curved one, at some cost in adaptability to uneven terrain.
