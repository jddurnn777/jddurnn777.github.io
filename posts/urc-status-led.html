Choosing a Status LED for Our URC Rover

Three ways to make a light visible in full daylight, and why blue is the hard color.

Tldr; I was assigned the Status LED for our University Rover Challenge rover: a light on the back that shows red, blue or flashing green depending on what the rover is doing, and has to be visible in full desert sun. I researched three designs. Option 1, a 16×16 addressable RGB matrix, is the cheapest (about $95–145) and most flexible, but blue comes in under my brightness target, it needs the most control parts, and it requires precise timing. Option 2, high-power LED strings, is the brightest and simplest to wire (about $214), but has a narrow beam and fails "on" if a control wire comes loose. Option 3, emergency-vehicle lightheads, is the lowest risk but costs about five times as much ($384–555) and I can't confirm its brightness until Whelen sends data. Right now I'm leaning toward Option 1 with a sunshade, with Option 2 as the backup.

Where this stands

What URC is

The University Rover Challenge (URC) is a yearly robotics competition run by the Mars Society, where university teams design and build a rover that would be useful to early explorers on Mars. It's been running since 2006, and the final competition is held at the Mars Desert Research Station near Hanksville, Utah, which was picked because its terrain and soil are similar to Mars.

Rovers compete in several missions. The exact set changes from year to year, but they've included things like a science mission (analyzing soil for signs of life), retrieving and delivering objects across rough terrain, doing hands-on tasks on mock equipment with a robotic arm, and an autonomous navigation mission where the rover has to drive itself to targets with no one driving it. Teams also have to qualify through written design reviews before they're invited to the final event.

That autonomous mission is why the Status LED exists. When a rover is driving itself, judges and anyone nearby need to be able to tell at a glance whether it's being driven by a person or by its own software, and whether it thinks it's reached its goal.

My assignment

I was assigned the Status LED design. The original task list was:

  • Figure out whether a power regulator is needed (step-up, step-down or both).
  • Look at how it would connect to our controller, the Jetson.
  • See whether the LED array can run on 12 V (preferable), and check the current ratings for the regulator.

That turned into a much bigger comparison once I realized that "visible in bright daylight" is the hard part, and that every option has a different weak spot.

What the rules actually require

URC requires an LED indicator on the back of the rover, visible in bright daylight (for example an LED array or high-power LED), that signals rover state by color:

  • Red: autonomous operation.
  • Blue: teleoperation (someone manually driving).
  • Flashing green: successful arrival at a target.

Two other rules shape the design a lot. All competition events are held in full daylight. And the rover's E-stop has to immediately stop the rover and cut power from every battery to every system on it. So whatever I build has to actually go dark when the E-stop fires, and I don't want a design that can fail "on."

There also used to be a minimum size for the indicator (8 cm × 8 cm, from a 2022 Q&A). That's not a requirement anymore, but I've kept it as a reference for sizing.

The electrical context I'm designing into

  • The battery: a 4S6P LiFePO4 pack, 12.8 V nominal and 36 Ah (about 460 Wh). Its voltage swings from about 14.6 V fully charged down to around 10 V in normal use, and the battery management system (BMS) cuts power at 8.8 V.
  • The power distribution board (PDB): its 12 V outputs aren't regulated. They float with the battery. The only regulator on our power diagram is the one feeding the Jetson, a Pololu S13V25F12.
  • The controller: a Jetson Orin Nano. Its 40-pin header uses 3.3 V logic and also has some 5 V pins.
  • The environment: desert heat, dust and possibly rain, so the light needs a mount that protects it.

That unregulated 12 V line ends up being one of the biggest differences between the three options. Some LEDs need a steady 12 V, and some don't care.

My targets, and why blue is the hard color

Two ways to measure "bright"

I set targets for two different kinds of brightness, because they answer different questions:

  • Luminance (nits, or cd/m²): how bright the lit surface looks up close. A screen needs at least about 1,500 nits to be readable in direct sun, and outdoor displays are usually spec'd at 4,500 cd/m² or more. So I'm aiming past 1,500 nits, with 4,500 as the stretch target.
  • Intensity (candela, cd): how visible the light is from far away, where it just looks like a dot. I'm targeting over 80 cd per color, based on the U.S. minimum for a car brake light (FMVSS 108), which is designed to be seen in daylight. For comparison, taillights are only 2–18 cd, because they're designed to be seen at night.

Why blue is always the problem

Candela is measured based on how sensitive the human eye is to each color. Our eyes are most sensitive to green and least sensitive to blue. So even if a blue LED puts out a similar amount of power, it counts for a lot less candela, and it looks dimmer to a person. That's why blue ends up being the hardest of the three colors to hit a visibility target with, no matter which hardware I pick, and it's the color I kept checking first for every option.

How I got to three options

My first idea was a small 8×8 WS2815 matrix (8 cm × 8 cm, 64 LEDs) with its own small controller, a Raspberry Pi Pico 2, plus a level shifter. My early conclusion was that a 12 V addressable matrix with a small dedicated controller made more sense than powering a 5 V LED matrix directly from the Jetson. As I worked through the brightness math, I sized up to a 16×16 panel, moved the control onto the Jetson, and added two completely different approaches to compare against it.

Option 1 — an addressable RGB matrix (WS2815)

The design

A 16×16 WS2815 panel: 256 RGB LEDs in a 16 cm × 16 cm square, with a 120° viewing angle, and every LED individually controllable in software. The panel gets power from a PDB output, through a 5 A inline fuse, into a Pololu S13V25F12 regulator, whose steady 12 V output feeds the panel. A 1000 µF capacitor sits across the panel's power input to smooth out current spikes. For control, the Jetson sends color data from its SPI MOSI pin, through a 74AHCT125 level shifter and a 330 Ω series resistor, into the panel's data input.

Why it needs a regulator

The panel's datasheet range is 9.5–13.5 V, but our pack runs from 8.8 to 14.6 V. That goes both above and below the panel's range, so a simple step-down or step-up regulator wouldn't be enough. The S13V25F12 is a buck-boost regulator: it holds 12 V (±4%) whether the pack is above or below 12 V. It's also the same regulator the Jetson already uses, which keeps the parts list simpler. It's rated for about 2.5 A, but the real limit depends on heat, so it needs airflow.

Why it needs a level shifter

Essentially the level shifter is required because the Jetson is sending timed data to the LEDs, not just turning a pin on and off like in normal embedded systems. Each WS2815 chip runs its logic at 5 V internally, and its datasheet sets the "high" threshold at 70% of that: 0.7 × 5 V = 3.5 V. The Jetson's header outputs 3.3 V, and that's the best case, which is 0.2 V short. It might work without the shifter by luck, but why take that chance on a competition run? The 74AHCT125 reads 3.3 V as a clear high (its threshold is 2.0 V) and outputs a full 5 V, so every pulse is clearly readable.

The timing problem, and the SPI trick

This is the part that makes Option 1 the most complex. The WS2815 reads data from one wire in pulses at 800,000 bits per second, so each bit is 1.25 µs long. The panel tells a 0 from a 1 by how long the pulse is: a short high pulse is a 0, and a long one (about 0.8 µs) is a 1. Each pixel needs 24 bits (8 each for green, red and blue), and there are 256 pixels.

The problem is that Linux on the Jetson can't reliably toggle a pin with sub-microsecond precision. So instead, the SPI hardware generates the pulses. Each LED bit gets sent as 3 SPI bits: 100 makes a short pulse (a 0), and 110 makes a long pulse (a 1). Running SPI at 2.4 MHz makes each group of 3 SPI bits last exactly 1.25 µs. One full frame is 256 pixels × 24 bits × 3 = 2,304 bytes.

My main concern with this is that every pulse has to be read correctly, thousands of times a second. A 3.3 V signal is already under the threshold, and each edge takes time to rise, so a pulse can look shorter than it really is or not register at all. A single misread bit shifts the colors on every pixel after it. The shifter fixes the voltage side, and the software side keeps things simple: only resend a frame when the rover's state changes, and make flashing green by alternating a green frame and an off frame at 1–2 Hz.

Power

  • Worst case, every LED at full white: 256 × 0.3 W = 76.8 W, which is 6.4 A at 12 V. That's way over what the regulator can handle.
  • But only one color should ever be lit. One color is roughly a third of full white, about 2.1 A, so I'd cap the current at 2.0 A in firmware. At that cap the panel still runs at nearly full brightness for a single color. I'd want to measure this on the bench to confirm it.
  • From the battery, 24 W out of the regulator at about 90% efficiency is about 2.1 A at 12.8 V, rising to 2.7 A when the pack is low.

Heat and weather

The panel isn't sealed, so it goes in a sealed, dust-tight and waterproof Hammond enclosure with a clear lid (180 × 180 × 60 mm), with cable glands where the power and data cables go in, and a vent screw to stop the lid from fogging up. (One detail I learned: cable glands only seal around round jacketed cable. Loose single wires will leak.)

I would be concerned about the heat. At the 2 A cap, the panel is putting out about 24 W, and a box like this warms up about 1.3 °C per watt, so that's roughly 30 °C of heating inside the box before the sun even hits it. The most at-risk parts are the panel and the level shifter, both rated to 85 °C. Inside the box they'd see about 35 °C from the desert air, plus about 30 °C from the LEDs, plus sunlight. That's a minimum of around 65 °C, which is fairly close to 85 °C, so this def shows we'd need a sunshade with this option.

For the sunshade, a cap-style hood with a 170 × 170 mm interior, 80–100 mm deep and open at the bottom would fit the panel. I'd want our mechanical team to make it. An 8″ traffic-signal visor would also work and is cheaper, but it shades the panel's corners.

Cost

About $78 before the enclosure and mounting, and about $125–145 total with them. With a purchased traffic-signal visor instead of a custom hood, it comes down to around $95. The panel itself is only $26.10.

Option 2 — high-power LED strings (Mean Well LDD-700H + Cree XP-E2)

The design

This is the electrically simpler path. Power comes from a PDB output, through a 5 A inline fuse, into three Mean Well LDD-700H constant-current drivers wired in parallel, one per color. Each driver powers a string of two Cree XP-E2 LEDs in series at a fixed 700 mA: one red string, one green and one blue. All six LEDs are mounted on an 80 × 80 mm aluminum plate that spreads their heat, each under a 20 mm Carclo wide-spot lens. For control, each driver's DIM pin connects straight to a Jetson GPIO pin, with a 10 kΩ pulldown resistor to ground. No regulator, level shifter, MOSFETs or LED resistors needed.

Why it doesn't need a regulator

The LDD-700H is essentially a regulator itself. It holds the LED current at 700 mA anywhere across its 9–52 V input range, and our pack stays inside that range except for the last 0.2 V before the BMS cuts off. The LED strings only need at most about 5.2 V (red) to 7.6 V (green), which leaves the driver plenty of headroom at the pack's typical 12.8 V. Near the very bottom of the pack, green might dim slightly, but that only happens right before the BMS shuts everything off anyway. No regulator also means one less part to overheat or fail.

The brightness math

  • Flux: at 350 mA the LEDs put out about 67.2 lm (red), 107 lm (green) and 30.6 lm (blue). At 700 mA, Cree's curve gives about 1.7× that: 114, 182 and 52 lm.
  • Intensity: the Carclo lens datasheet gives a peak of 2.50 cd per lumen. So each LED is flux × 2.50, and there are 2 LEDs per color.
  • Luminance: intensity divided by the lit area. Two 20 mm lenses is about 6.28 × 10⁻⁴ m², which is tiny, so the luminance numbers come out huge.

Numerically, this is the strongest option. All three colors clear both brightness targets, and blue is the brightest of all three options here, just because a dedicated high-power LED beats spreading the same power across lots of small ones.

The catch: a narrow beam

The lens is what makes it so bright, and it's also the tradeoff. The beam is 31.5° wide at half brightness, so the light drops to half at about ±16° off-center. Even at half brightness, blue is still 130 cd, but someone off to the side sees a lot less. At 10 m behind the rover, ±16° only covers about ±2.8 m to either side. That's a much narrower cone than Option 1's 120°.

I looked at two ways around that, and neither works. Adding a diffuser to make the light look bigger spreads it back out, and blue drops to 33 cd, which fails. Going without lenses (bare LEDs have a 130° beam) gives red 73 cd, green 116 cd and blue 33 cd, so red and blue fail. The lenses are required.

The catch: it fails "on"

The driver's DIM pin turns the color on when it's left floating. While the Jetson is booting, its pins float, so all three colors could light up at once. The 10 kΩ pulldown should hold them off, but I'd want to test that on the bench. And if a DIM wire comes loose, that color turns on and stays on. That's the opposite of what I'd want next to an E-stop requirement, so the software also needs to drive all three pins low at startup before the mode logic runs.

Power, weather and cost

Worst case is green: 2 LEDs × 3.8 V × 0.7 A = 5.3 W, or about 5.9 W from the pack with driver losses. That's only 0.46 A from the battery (0.66 A when the pack is low), and about 1.3% of the pack's energy for an hour of use. The drivers are fully encapsulated (IP67), and the LED plate just needs a clear polycarbonate dust cover and a sunshade. A 7″ monitor hood fits the 80 mm plate with room to spare. The mount is more mechanically involved than Option 1, and the total comes to about $214.

Option 3 — vehicle-grade lightheads (Whelen ION)

The design

The lowest-engineering-risk option: pre-built Whelen ION surface-mount lightheads, one per color, the same class of hardware used on real emergency vehicles. Power comes from a PDB output through a 5 A fuse to all three heads. Each head's ground wire runs through its own IRLB8721 logic-level MOSFET to ground, and each MOSFET's gate is driven by a Jetson GPIO pin through a 100 Ω resistor, with a 100 kΩ pulldown so the light stays off while the Jetson boots. No regulator or level shifter needed, since these are built to run directly on a vehicle's 12 V system, which swings about as much as our pack does.

Why a MOSFET, and one wiring gotcha

A Jetson GPIO pin can't power a light directly, so the MOSFET acts as a switch: 3.3 V on the gate turns it on (the IRLB8721 is fully on by 2.35 V), which connects the head's ground and lights it. At 0.40 A per head, the MOSFET only wastes under 0.01 W, so it doesn't need a heatsink.

One thing I caught while working through this: since the MOSFET switches the ground wire, the heads' pattern and sync wires can't be tied to ground anywhere else. If they are, they could bypass the switch and leave the head on.

The catch: cost and missing data

The catch is cost, roughly five times either other option, at about $384 for the parts with listed prices and around $555 with the green head. And Whelen doesn't publish intensity, luminance or viewing angle numbers for these heads. They're certified to SAE J595, the standard for emergency-vehicle warning lights, which are designed to be seen in daylight. That's reassuring, but I can't check them against my own numbers until I get photometric data from Whelen directly.

There are a couple of other things to confirm before buying. The product page only mentions flash patterns, so we'd need to make sure the heads have a steady-on mode. The listed 0.40 A is an average across flash patterns, so steady-on might draw more. And I'd want to check Whelen's minimum input voltage, since if it's above 10 V, the heads could cut out near the bottom of the pack.

Where I'm leaning

Right now: Option 1, because it's the cheapest and most flexible, and it would work as long as we add the sunshade. Option 2 is an active fallback, not an afterthought. If the matrix's timing requirements turn out to be more fragile in practice than they look on paper, I want a simpler backup already scoped instead of starting from zero. Option 3 stays in reserve as the "spend more, worry less" option if we decide the risk on the other two isn't worth the savings.

Next steps

  • Request Whelen's photometric data so Option 3 is actually comparable on numbers.
  • Settle whether our mechanical team builds a sunshade before committing to Option 1's blue-channel margin.
  • Bench-test the parts I flagged: Option 1's current draw at the firmware cap, and Option 2's pulldowns holding the LEDs off while the Jetson boots.

What I've learned so far

The biggest thing is that "just add an LED" turns into a real systems problem once it has to work outdoors. Brightness depends on how the human eye sees color, the power setup depends on a battery whose voltage moves around, the control side depends on timing a Linux computer isn't great at, and the enclosure depends on heat. Every option trades one of those off against another. Writing down the numbers for each one is what made the tradeoffs clear enough to actually compare.
