Choosing a Status LED for Our URC Rover

Three ways to make a light visible in full daylight, and why blue is the hard color.
This one's still open: I got assigned the Status LED design for our University Rover Challenge (URC) rover, and I'm mid-decision, not finished. This is how I'm actually thinking through the tradeoffs right now. I'll update this post once the team lands on one.

What the rules actually require

URC requires an LED indicator on the back of the rover, visible in bright daylight, that signals rover state by color: red for autonomous operation, blue for teleoperation, and a flashing green for successful arrival at a target. Competition events run in full daylight, and the required E-stop has to immediately cut power to every battery-driven system on the rover, which matters for how I wire this. Whatever I build has to actually go dark when the E-stop fires, not fail "on."

The electrical context I'm designing into: our power distribution board's 12 V outputs aren't regulated, they float with the battery, a 4S6P LiFePO4 pack that runs roughly 10–14.6 V depending on charge state. The controller is a Jetson Orin Nano, so 3.3 V logic, with some 5 V pins available. And the enclosure needs to survive dust, rain, and rough desert conditions.

Why blue is the hard color

I'm targeting two things: luminance, how bright the lit surface actually looks (aiming past 1500 nits, with 4500 cd/m² as the outdoor-visibility reference point), and intensity per color, targeting over 80 candela, based roughly on the U.S. minimum brightness standard for a car brake light. Candela is the unit that actually accounts for human eye sensitivity by color, and blue is the color our eyes are least sensitive to. So it's consistently the hardest of the three to hit a visibility target with, no matter which hardware I pick.

Option 1 — an addressable RGB matrix (WS2815)

A 16×16 WS2815 panel is the cheapest and most software-flexible option, roughly $125–145 including mounting, with the widest viewing angle (120°) and full software control over color and pattern. The catch is it needs the most supporting hardware: a regulator, a level shifter, and precise firmware-side timing.

The level shifter isn't optional. The WS2815 reads its data line internally at 5 V logic, and its datasheet sets the high threshold at 70% of that, 3.5 V. The Jetson's SPI output is 3.3 V, which is 0.2 V short of that threshold. It might work by luck. I'd rather not bet a competition run on luck, so a 74AHCT125 shifter bumps the signal to a clean 5 V before it reaches the panel.

Since the Jetson can't reliably toggle a pin with sub-microsecond precision the way the WS2815 protocol needs, the SPI hardware generates the timing instead: each LED bit gets encoded as 3 SPI bits (short pulse = 0, long pulse = 1), sent at 2.4 MHz. Clever workaround, but also exactly the kind of "precise timing required" tradeoff I keep coming back to.

Weather-wise, the panel and level shifter are only rated to 85°C, and back-of-envelope heating math puts the sealed enclosure close enough to that limit in direct desert sun that a sunshade isn't optional for this option. It's load-bearing for the electronics surviving the day.

Against my targets: red and green clear the bar comfortably. Blue lands at roughly half the luminance target without a sunshade. Workable, but the option I'm least confident in for blue specifically.

Option 2 — high-power LED strings (Mean Well LDD-700H + Cree XP-E2)

This is the electrically simpler path: three Mean Well LDD-700H constant-current drivers, one per color, each driving a pair of Cree XP-E2 LEDs in series at a fixed 700 mA. No regulator, no level shifter, no MOSFETs. The driver itself holds current constant across the pack's whole voltage swing, and each driver's DIM pin reads the Jetson's 3.3 V GPIO output directly.

The tradeoffs are mechanical and behavioral rather than electrical. Each LED sits under a narrow wide-spot lens, so the usable viewing cone is much tighter than the matrix's 120°. Someone off to the side sees meaningfully less light. And because the driver's DIM pin turns the color on when left floating high, a loose control wire fails toward "on" rather than "off," which is the opposite of what I'd want near an E-stop requirement.

Against my targets, this is actually the strongest option numerically. All three colors clear both brightness targets, and blue is the brightest of all three options here, purely because a dedicated high-power LED beats spreading the same power across many small ones.

Option 3 — vehicle-grade lightheads (Whelen ION)

The lowest-engineering-risk option: pre-built Whelen ION surface-mount lightheads, one per color, the same class of hardware used on real emergency vehicles. Wiring is about as simple as it gets: power through a fuse, ground through a logic-level MOSFET switched by a Jetson GPIO. No regulator or level shifter needed since these are built to run directly on a vehicle's 12 V system, which our pack's voltage range fits inside.

The catch is cost, roughly five times either other option, and that Whelen doesn't publish intensity or luminance numbers for these heads. They're certified to SAE J595, the standard for emergency-vehicle warning lights, which is reassuring, but I can't check them against my own numeric targets until I actually get photometric data from Whelen directly.

Where I'm leaning

Right now: Option 1, because it's the cheapest and most flexible, with Option 2 as an active fallback rather than an afterthought. If the matrix's timing requirements turn out to be more fragile in practice than they look on paper, I want a simpler backup already scoped rather than starting from zero. Option 3 stays in reserve as the "spend more, worry less" option if we decide the risk on the other two isn't worth the savings.

Next concrete steps: request Whelen's photometric data so Option 3 is actually comparable on numbers, and settle whether a sunshade gets built by our mechanical subteam before I commit to Option 1's blue-channel margin.
